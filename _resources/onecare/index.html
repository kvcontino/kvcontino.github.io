--- layout: default title: "OneCare Vermont: An Accounting" description: "What the public data says Vermont's eight-year all-payer ACO experiment did — and did not — do." ---

For eight years Vermont ran the most ambitious all-payer payment experiment in the country, moving more than a billion dollars a year in provider payments through a single accountable care organization — though, as this accounting will show, at its peak only about four cents of every dollar Vermont spent on health care was ever paid in the way the model was built to pay it. It sunset at the end of 2025. This is an accounting of what the public data says it did — and did not — do.

By Kevin Contino · July 2026 · Every figure below is rebuilt from public data; the code, the source files, and a methodology note are linked throughout and collected at the end.

Two claims have competed over Vermont's all-payer model since it ended. One is that it was a transformation — that global payment bent the cost curve, moved care upstream, and left the system healthier. The other is that it was a boondoggle — an expensive administrative layer that enriched a hospital network and delivered nothing. The public data supports neither. What it shows is a model whose signature mechanism reached about four percent of the health-care dollar, and whose measurable effects at the point of care — a fragile spending signal, flat admissions, an ED trend that ran the wrong way for the model's own theory — are too small, and too diluted, to separate from ordinary noise at the state level.

That is a deflationary reading, and I want to be precise about what it does and does not say. It does not say the people who built and ran OneCare were foolish or idle; the administration was real, competent, and in its late years carried out under genuine strain. It does not say nothing good happened — the federal evaluation found rising primary-care contact, and that shows up in the data below. It says the mechanism the model is named for barely touched the Vermont health-care dollar, and that the design was built such that whether it worked or failed was, at the state level, largely unmeasurable. I built the case back up from primary sources so that the claim rests on something an adversary could check. The methodology note and the data are there for exactly that.

The spending question

The model's central financial promise was lower total cost of care. To test it without a single comparison state doing all the work, I built a synthetic Vermont — a weighted blend of other states, fit to match Vermont's pre-model Medicare spending trajectory, then carried forward as the counterfactual. The gap between real and synthetic Vermont after 2018 is the model's apparent effect.

One thing has to be held onto about what this estimand can mean, and it bears on every null below. Vermont was already a mature ACO state well before the treatment date. OneCare entered the Medicare Shared Savings Program in 2013 — the same payer as the outcome measured here, five years early. The state's commercial and Medicaid shared-savings programs began on January 1, 2014, the first year of the pre-period fit. And Vermont Medicaid Next Generation — the one mechanism this essay will conclude was ever real — launched in 2017, inside the pre-period, with four risk-bearing hospital communities and about 29,000 attributed lives. The line marked 2018 is not the line between reform and no reform. It is the line between one kind of ACO and a slightly different one.

That is measurable, so I measured it. CMS publishes assigned-beneficiary counts by county for every Shared Savings Program ACO; aggregating those to state of residence and dividing by each state's Medicare fee-for-service population gives a like-for-like ACO penetration rate. Vermont entered the model as the second-most ACO-saturated state in the country.

In 2017, 60,480 of Vermont's 117,186 fee-for-service beneficiaries were already assigned to an ACO — behind only Delaware. Its synthetic control sat at about 25%. That comparison is if anything too kind to my own argument, because the Shared Savings Program was not the only ACO game: Maine, which carries the second-largest donor weight, also ran a Next Generation ACO, which this file does not cover. Vermont ran none — no Next Generation, no Pioneer — so Vermont's figure is complete while the donors' are understated. Adding Maine's Next Generation population back lifts synthetic Vermont to about 26%. Vermont went into 2018 some twenty-five percentage points more ACO-penetrated than its own counterfactual — roughly twice as saturated. It is also worth seeing how unlike each other the donors are on this measure: Delaware sits at 53%, Wyoming under 7%. Whatever the donor blend is matching Vermont on, it is not accountable-care exposure.

Nor is this a late-arriving feature of the pre-period. The county files begin in 2016, but the ACO-level results files reach back further, and splitting each ACO's beneficiaries by its own observed state mix carries Vermont's series back to the start of the fit window. That method is worth one sentence of defence, because the obvious alternative is wrong: splitting a multi-state ACO in proportion to state population puts only a third of OneCare in Vermont, when OneCare is about 95% Vermont, and it misses the true 2016 figure by 29 points. Splitting by each ACO's own mix instead — learned from 2016 alone, then tested against 2017, a year the fitting never saw — lands within 0.5 points for Vermont and 1.8 nationally. On that footing Vermont ran 61% in 2014 and 54% in 2015, against a synthetic control near 15% in both years. Vermont's ACO penetration never fell below 45% in any year the synthetic control was fitted on. The pre-period this design treats as clean baseline is nothing of the kind.

Three consequences follow, and I want all three on the table. First, the estimate below is not "the model versus no reform" but the increment of the all-payer wrapper over an ACO baseline — attenuated before any data was collected, so a null against it is weaker evidence than a null against an untreated comparison. Second, and cutting the other way, the specification this essay uses assumes that pre-existing gap away. Matching Vermont's pre-period shape and shifting the counterfactual onto Vermont's own level treats the level offset as a nuisance — but if any part of it is accumulated pre-2018 ACO effect rather than the donor pool's failure to reach Vermont's low spending, the specification is subtracting real signal along with the nuisance. I lean to the donor-pool explanation as the more parsimonious one, and the essay's estimate is smaller for having made that choice. It is a choice, not a finding, and it is the single assumption most worth attacking.

Third — and this one is not a caveat but a finding. If the all-payer model was a step change in accountable care, the count should jump in 2018. It does not. Vermont's Shared Savings Program number falls off a cliff that year, but only because OneCare left that program for the all-payer model's own Medicare track, which CMS reports separately; the beneficiaries did not go anywhere. Put both regimes on one denominator and Vermont ran 51.6% of its fee-for-service population inside an ACO in 2017 and 41.5% in 2020, the model's third performance year. It did not rise. The federal evaluation's more familiar 57.5% is a share of attribution-eligible beneficiaries, a narrower denominator, and the two numbers must not be set side by side without that restatement. On a consistent count, joining the most ambitious payment experiment in the country did not put a single additional Vermonter inside an accountable care organization. It changed the contract governing people who were already in one — which is the same finding the scale targets and the four cents reach by two other routes.

The savings replicate in direction and not in significance, and the estimate remains fragile in a way the specification does not fix. Vermont sits near the bottom of the national spending distribution and no blend of donors reaches its level, so the fit here matches the shape of Vermont's pre-period and shifts the counterfactual onto Vermont's own level — the intercept-shifted specification described in the methodology note, which replaced an earlier one that matched on levels and fit far worse. That buys a great deal of precision and it hides a problem: held out of its own fit, the spending model predicts 2017 with an error of $103 while reporting an in-sample error of $3. It understates its own uncertainty roughly thirty-sevenfold. The honest noise floor for this outcome is the $103, not the $3, and every sensitivity claim below is built on the former. Of the three outcomes this is the one whose fit generalises worst — a hostile analyst would seize on it, so I am flagging it myself.

There is a fourth problem, and it cuts the other way from how it first reads. Netted against the pre-period, the −$351 gap is roughly 4% of Medicare spending — and that is a gross figure, before any side payments, from a mechanism that reached four cents of every dollar. The benchmark that matters is hospital-led ACOs, because that is what OneCare is. In the Shared Savings Program, McWilliams and colleagues found that hospital-integrated ACOs' spending reductions were offset entirely by their bonus payments; only the physician-group ACOs netted Medicare anything — $256M in 2015, on $583M of gross reductions. The Next Generation ACO model, the most on-point benchmark of all, is starker still: over six years it cut gross Parts A and B spending by $1.7 billion — a real reduction, significant at p<0.01 — and paid out $1.8 billion in shared savings to get it, leaving Medicare $97M worse off (+0.1%, not significant) by the model's end. CMS discontinued it at the end of 2021. That is the whole problem with reading a gross gap as a verdict: a model with far stronger incentives than Vermont's bought $1.7B of real savings and handed every dollar of it back. A gross signal several times larger than mature programs produce, from a mechanism several times weaker, is not a triumph to bank; it is prima facie evidence that the estimate is catching something other than the model — most of it, as the utilization section will show, arriving after 2020. The honest summary is a small, favorable, and untrustworthy spending signal.

The utilization promise

Total spending is a blunt instrument. The theory underneath it is specific: an ACO paid to keep a population healthy should move care upstream — fewer emergency-department visits, fewer avoidable admissions, more primary care. That is a directional prediction, and it is checkable against the same synthetic control. Put spending, inpatient stays, and emergency-department visits on one scale and the ED series is the one that moved.

Vermont's emergency-department use diverged from its counterfactual, and the divergence is real: p = 0.04, second-most-extreme of fifty states, on the outcome whose fit generalises best — held out of its own estimation the ED model predicts 2017 almost exactly, so its precision is earned rather than borrowed. This is the one place in the statewide data where something clearly happened. It also runs opposite to the direction the model's theory predicts. An organization paid to manage a population was supposed to move care upstream and out of the emergency department; Vermont's ED rate went the other way, and kept going after the model ended its scale-up.

What the finding establishes is a divergence, not its cause. Two rival explanations are live, and both would produce this pattern without any help from the payment model — so the section below is about which of the three is doing the work, not about whether the result counts.

The federal evaluation finds the same direction in the attributed population. NORC's own beneficiary-level measure for the attributed Medicare population — ED visits and observation stays combined — also rose, by 40.7 per 1,000 (about 8% above baseline), non-significant (p = 0.32), directionally consistent with the statewide climb here. Within NORC's hospital-and-ED block, the only significant result was a fall in acute inpatient stays (−35.6 per 1,000, p < 0.10) — and part of that decline is deliberate substitution of observation stays for admissions, which lands inside the ED-and-observation measure. So flat inpatient counts alongside a rising ED-and-observation count is partly the admission-to-observation signature that ACO theory predicts as a success. The testable promise the data can see (all ED visits) is coarser than the promise the theory makes (avoidable ED visits); the coarser measure cannot cleanly separate the two. The relevant point for the statewide result is that the attributed-population evaluation moves the same way: two independent designs, on different populations with different comparators, both find Vermont's emergency-department contact rising. That is corroboration, and it is why the divergence should be taken as real rather than as an artifact of one specification.

One NORC result sits outside that block and is larger than anything in it, so it belongs here rather than in a footnote: specialty-care evaluation-and-management visits fell by 3,370 per 1,000 beneficiary-years against a baseline of 7,615 — a 44% drop, and the most significant estimate in the evaluation at p = 0.000. It is filed under care delivery rather than hospital utilization, which is why the sentence above is scoped the way it is, but the section's own framing of the promise — "more primary care" — puts it in play. It admits two readings and the data does not choose between them. Read one way it is the model's theory working: care moving off the specialty ladder is what an ACO paid to manage a population is supposed to produce. Read the other, it is reduced access to specialists in a state with known workforce shortages, which is the moderate version of the rationing critique and a good deal harder to dismiss than the 20,000-deaths version taken apart below. Two things keep either reading from being banked. Primary-care E&M visits rose in the same table but not significantly (+1,255.8, p = 0.240), so the substitution the favorable reading needs is not established; and ambulatory-care-sensitive hospitalizations — the direct measure of the avoidable admissions both readings imply should move — sat at +0.1 per 1,000, p = 0.971. Something real happened to specialty contact. Neither the essay nor NORC can say what it did to anyone.

Rival one: the workforce and boarding crisis. A modest gap was already visible in 2018–19 (about +29 per 1,000), but the divergence roughly doubles after 2020 and the post-2020 years account for 86% of the cumulative total — squarely the window of the national and Vermont-specific ED-boarding and workforce crisis, which NORC names as its own explanation. There is also a denominator problem the synthetic control cannot reach. The spending and ED series are Medicare fee-for-service only, and Vermont's FFS denominator shrank 16.5% from its 2019 peak as Medicare Advantage grew — the UVM Health Network co-launched a Medicare Advantage plan (UVM Health Advantage, with MVP) in January 2022, and Vermont's MA penetration crossed above its synthetic comparator's in exactly that year. If healthier beneficiaries are the ones leaving FFS, the shrinking denominator mechanically inflates per-capita FFS spending and ED rates with no change in real utilization. Robustness checks that match MA penetration in the pre-period move the estimate by less than 2% — but by construction they cannot reach a divergence that opens up entirely after 2021, which is exactly where it would hide. Call this rival two: a compositional artifact rather than a change in anyone's behaviour. Neither rival is idle, and neither is established. The workforce explanation has to account for the gap that was already open in 2018–19, before the crisis; the denominator explanation has to account for the divergence continuing to widen through 2024, well after the initial Medicare Advantage shift. Both are more parsimonious than a payment mechanism reaching four cents of the dollar, which is the third candidate and the weakest of them.

The finer measure the theory actually names points the other way. This essay has said twice that all-ED-visits is a coarser thing than the avoidable-ED-visits the model promised to reduce, and that the coarser measure cannot separate them. Vermont's all-payer claims database can. A 2023 analysis of VHCURES commissioned by the Green Mountain Care Board classified every Vermont emergency-department visit from 2017 to 2021 using the NYU avoidability algorithm, and the avoidable share fell for every payer: 34% to 29% for Medicare, 38% to 31% for Medicaid, 32% to 27% for commercial, with total avoidable-ED spending growth running about −2% a year. Put that beside the finding above and the picture changes shape. Vermont's total Medicare ED rate was roughly flat from 2017 to 2024 (662 to 672 per 1,000) while its synthetic comparator's fell — that divergence is what the p = 0.04 is measuring — but within those visits, the share the model was actually supposed to prevent was declining. Multiply the two through and avoidable ED visits per Vermont beneficiary come out close to flat, with the rise concentrated in visits no care-coordination programme claims to avert.

Two limits on that, both real. The VHCURES series has no comparison group — it is Vermont against its own past, the exact target-versus-baseline construction this essay criticises elsewhere, and avoidable-ED shares were falling nationally over the same window. And it stops in 2021, before the largest part of the divergence above accrues. So it cannot exonerate the model, and I am not claiming it does. What it does is remove the strongest available reading of the ED result: the statewide series was never evidence that the model failed on its own stated margin, because on that margin, measured directly, Vermont moved the way the theory wanted. The honest summary is that Vermont's emergency departments got busier relative to comparators, that the increase was not in avoidable visits, and that nobody has run the comparison that would say why.

Two statistical qualifications, stated once. Three outcomes were tested and this is the section on the one that came out most extreme; a p of 0.04 in three tries corresponds to roughly a one-in-nine chance under the global null. And this retrospective synthetic control was not pre-registered, and its specification was revised once after the first results were seen — disclosed in the appendix because a reader should be able to weigh it. Neither qualification makes the divergence go away; both mean it should be carried as a strong signal awaiting an out-of-sample test rather than as a settled fact. The resolution is pre-registered as hypothesis H5 — if the gap was model-caused, removing the model should bend it; continued growth on trend exonerates the model on this margin.

Who the model actually reached

Any reader who has worked with ACOs will look first for the number the essay above has been circling: how many Vermonters were actually attributed to OneCare, and how much of the state did that leave out. It matters because a real per-attributed-life effect is diluted two to three times over before it reaches a statewide Medicare series — which is both why the synthetic control could miss a genuine effect and why the effect it does show should be read as smaller than the model's own internal estimates.

Attribution grew from about 110,000 lives (22% of eligible) in 2018 to a peak of 259,958 (50%) in 2022, then went backwards: Blue Cross Blue Shield of Vermont withdrew for 2023, erasing roughly 93,000 lives — about a third of the portfolio — so the commercial "all-payer" leg effectively collapsed before the model's scheduled end. Even the payer with real prospective payment reached only about half its own population: in 2020, OneCare's 49,337 attributed Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries were 57.5% of the 85,792 living in-state — and that FFS denominator was itself shrinking as Medicare Advantage grew. The churn was not only at the payer level; fourteen independent primary-care practices moved to quit in August 2020 over a cut to the per-member payment, resolved only by preserving the payment through 2021.

Put the dilution in one line. A real effect concentrated in the attributed population — roughly half of Medicare, none of commercial in payment terms — is averaged across a statewide series in which the other half moves on its own. The federal evaluation's estimand (attributed lives, matched to out-of-state ACOs) and this essay's (all Vermont fee-for-service) are therefore not interchangeable: dilution predicts the statewide estimate should be the smaller of the two, and it is. This is the arithmetic underneath the four-cents finding two sections down.

The model's own scorecard

Before grading the model against an outside counterfactual, it is worth scoring it against its own contract — because the state's official scoreboard reaches a version of the same conclusion, and citing it converts "my strict definition" into "the model's own numbers." The All-Payer Model Agreement set three kinds of target.

On the Medicare growth target — per-beneficiary total cost of care growing 0.2 points below projected national Medicare growth — the model met the target every one of its five performance years (NORC, Fourth Report, p.26; in PY5 Vermont ran 6.6 to 8.4 points below its Medicare targets). That is the model's own scorecard recording a genuine hit, and it belongs on the page. On the all-payer growth target — 3.5% compound annual growth, the binding metric assessed at model end — the annual figures that are public are mixed (2018 came in at 4.1%, within the 4.3% enforcement threshold; 2019 near 4.6%, above the 3.5% goal), and the final compound verdict was not confirmed to a single citable GMCB statement in this pass, so I state only what is documented and no more. On the scale targets — the share of Vermonters and of Medicare beneficiaries the model had to enroll — it fell short throughout, and CMS waived enforcement in October 2021, deeming the targets unattainable.

That scale failure is the state's own official version of the four-cents finding: by the government's own scoreboard the model never reached the reach it was built to reach. An independent count agrees. Measured against all Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries rather than the narrower attribution-eligible population, Vermont's ACO-enrolled share ran 51.6% the year before the model began and 41.5% in 2020 — the model did not expand accountable care in Medicare so much as re-paper it. The scale targets and the federal ACO assignment files, compiled by different people for different purposes, land in the same place. What the scoreboard cannot do is credit the growth hit as an effect. The growth targets were set against Vermont's own projected baseline, not against what comparable states actually did, and with no comparator a target beaten is a projection beaten — which is why the synthetic control, not the scorecard, carries the causal question.

Population health: the targets met themselves

Vermont's model was reported, year after year, as meeting its population-health targets. Both halves of that sentence are true and only one is informative, because the targets were set against Vermont's own projected baseline rather than against what comparable states actually did. Measure Vermont against its Northeast peers instead, and the story on the two flagship causes — overdose and suicide — is not the deterioration the failure case wants, nor the success the target regime claims. It is mostly a pre-existing gap the model did not touch.

Take the two causes in turn, because they fail the model's target regime in different ways. Suicide is a level difference, not a trend: Vermont has run several points per 100,000 above its peers since around 2010 — eight years before the model — and the gap did not widen during the model window (+5.2 immediately before, +5.5 during, age-adjusted, well inside the year-to-year noise). A level that predates the model cannot indict it; by the same token, a suicide "target" scored against Vermont's own baseline cannot credit it. Overdose is a genuine deterioration relative to peers — the multi-year climb survives the Poisson noise in the counts — but the overdose curve is a fentanyl curve, and no one credible thinks an accountable care organization could have stopped a fentanyl wave. That cuts against counting the target regime as success exactly as much as it cuts against blaming the model for the deaths. Both flagship targets, in other words, are scored on quantities the model neither moved nor was built to move.

Two further notes belong here. The chronic-disease and access measures from BRFSS show no divergence from peers at all — nothing got visibly worse — and the one genuinely favorable comparative signal is that Vermont's share of adults reporting a personal doctor pulled further ahead of peers after 2021, consistent with NORC's finding of rising primary-care contact. I want that on the ledger, with two caveats stated in the same breath: the personal-doctor question was redesigned in the 2021 BRFSS cycle (PERSDOC2 to PERSDOC3), so the comparison is of gaps, not levels; and Vermont's Blueprint for Health has been paying for primary-care medical homes for a decade, which confounds the primary-care signal with the model at least as plausibly as it credits it. That is why Blueprint enters the pre-registered hypotheses below as a rival explanation, not a footnote.

What the data will not support is the strongest version of the failure claim either. The figure of "20,000 premature deaths" that has attached itself to the Vermont experiment fails arithmetic outright: Vermont's all-cause mortality runs about 6% above its regional peers on an age-adjusted basis (VSRR, 2023: 740.8 vs. a peer mean of 698.5 per 100,000) and below the national rate — the crude excess a second-oldest state would show anyway is not the point — and 20,000 excess deaths over eight years would be roughly a third of all deaths in the state, every year, unmissable in any dataset. One more caution belongs on every panel here: mortality and chronic disease respond to delivery-system change on a 5-to-15-year lag, so the utilization window is adequate to the model but the mortality window is not, whatever the design. The population-health case for the model is empty; the population-health case against it is overstated. Both statements are the same kind of statement — a refusal to read a number as more than it is.

One hearing, five hours

Everything above is aggregate. It is worth stopping once in a room, because the central finding of this essay was said out loud in one — under oath, by the people who ran the model — four years before I went looking for it in the data.

On November 9, 2022, OneCare came before the Green Mountain Care Board for its fiscal year 2023 budget hearing. The board was newly constituted and its chair, Owen Foster, opened with an admonition rather than a welcome:

To my eye, the fiscal year 2022 presentation by OneCare was not particularly well-focused. It was long on process and light on demonstrable results. […] So I ask OneCare to please concentrate your remarks on objectively showing the impact OneCare has had, not through one-off anecdotes but quantifiable metrics and analysis that tie back to OneCare's work.

He added that the witnesses were under oath, and that "obfuscation or misleading responses are detrimental to this board's review and the process." Five hours later the board had not gotten what it asked for. The exchange that matters most for this essay took two questions:

MR. FOSTER: And in terms of shifting people to value-based care, it looks like only one payer is actually doing unreconciled fixed-perspective payment. Is that right?
MS. LONER: That is correct.
MR. FOSTER: So how would investing more in population health increase that output?
MS. LONER: So I think of the two things as very different. You're talking about fixed, predictable payments. […] That is, indeed, only being offered by one payer, Medicaid, right now. The state doesn't have a means to force commercial payers to enter into fixed arrangements. And through the agreement with the state, Medicare has signaled to the state through that agreement that they will not offer fixed prospective payments.

That is the chief executive of the all-payer model's only ACO confirming, in year five of eight, that the all-payer model had one payer. The next section is arithmetic performed on that answer. Later in the same hearing a board member read the number straight off page 22 of OneCare's own submission: of $306 million in Medicaid total cost of care, "only 171 million's unreconciled" — the rest still billed fee-for-service. Nobody in the room disputed it. OneCare's finance vice-president explained the gap and moved on.

The rest of Loner's answer deserves more than the quotation marks I have put around it, because it is the best defense available to the model and it comes from the witness. She named two constraints, neither of which was hers. The state had no means to compel commercial payers into fixed arrangements — a limit that traces to the 1985 Supreme Court decision holding self-funded employer plans beyond the reach of state insurance law, which is why "all-payer" was voluntary for the payers that mattered most. And Medicare, through the very agreement that created the model, had signalled it would not offer fixed prospective payment. Take both seriously and four cents stops being a story about an organization that failed to execute and becomes a story about a ceiling set by the federal counterparty and by federal law, with the state holding an instrument that could only reach Medicaid. That reframing survives everything else in this essay, and it should change who the finding is about. It does not rescue the design: a state that accepts a payment-reform mandate it has no authority to deliver, and then spends eight years and nine figures administering the fraction it can reach, has still made a choice worth auditing. But the choice was made in 2016, by the people who signed an agreement without a rate provision — not in the years afterward by the people asked to explain it.

What the board could not get was evidence. Asked for OneCare's top three key performance indicators — six years in — the answer was that ten or twelve were with the UVM health-services research team and headed to OneCare's own board: "I don't want to be in front of our governance process in saying what those final measures are." Thom Walsh, a board member, put the standard as plainly as it can be put:

If there was a mature service organization following outcomes and working to improve processes, we'd see tables and charts of where things were at the beginning, what's the — the current system performance, what interventions have we utilized, and what's the performance now? […] I — I don't see things like that in your submission. I see a lot of different grass from a lot of different places and a lot of reference to federal government things.

Walsh was also, in 2022, looking at the same two signals this essay reconstructs in 2026: he noted that suicides were at an all-time high in Vermont and that OneCare's emergency-department visits ran "twenty-nine to thirty-seven percent above those of comparison ACOs." The figures were on the record the whole time. What the board wanted was somebody to connect them to the money, and what it got, on the question of timeframe, was this:

[…] how do you manage these one-year payer-contract cycles and performance expectations with mid- and long-term outcomes that our clinicians remind us all the time it's going to take years, decades, generations to address.

Spoken by OneCare's chief operating officer, per VTDigger's contemporaneous report. The official transcript labels the speaker as a board member, which the content contradicts — "all levels of our governance," "we as the staff at OneCare." I read that as a transcription error and name no speaker above.

I read this transcript in 2023 and came away with five questions. The third was the one that stuck, and it is the one this essay exists to answer: what percentage of Vermont health-care dollars actually moves under the arrangement being described? I want to be careful about what the generational answer does and does not show, because the easy reading is the wrong one. It is not evidence of bad faith. Delivery-system change really is slow, and the mortality caveat above concedes as much on the measures where it applies. What it is, is a defense with no expiry — and it sits oddly beside what OneCare said its own work was. Asked in that same hearing what the organization actually did, Loner named three core capabilities: "the contracting, the data and analytics, and the payment reform mechanisms." None of those is a generational project. All three can be reported year over year. Asked next whether she had "any sort of hierarchy or basis to opine on which are the most effective at curbing costs," she answered: "I don't know that I could answer that question." Set that beside a mechanism reaching four cents of the dollar and the shape of the problem is clear. The model was hard to evaluate because of how it was built, and the language around it made not evaluating it comfortable.

Four cents on the dollar

So: the model's premise — providers freed from fee-for-service, paid prospectively to manage a population — describes about four percent of Vermont health-care spending at its peak. Everything above is measured against a mechanism that, in dollar terms, barely existed.

This is not a new finding and I should not present it as one. Katie Jickling ran the same division for VTDigger in April 2021, five years before this essay: about 13% of Vermont health-care spending flowed through OneCare, of which only 13% arrived as fixed monthly payments — "less than 2% overall." Loner confirmed then that Medicaid comprised the entire figure. Kevin Mullin, chairing the Green Mountain Care Board, called it "abysmal" and said the state was "way behind" a 25% expectation. It was never concealed: the regulator published it, a reporter divided it out and printed it in 2021, and the operator confirmed the arithmetic under oath in 2022. What was missing was never the number. It was somebody willing to set it next to the outcome claims and say that the second cannot mean much given the first.

Two things have to be said before that column is allowed to carry any weight, because a hostile reader pulling the same budget orders will find both. Four cents is the strictest of three defensible definitions, and I chose it. Counting Medicare's all-inclusive population-based payment — prospective in cash flow, reconciled to fee-for-service at settlement — roughly doubles the figure to 8–9%. Counting everything OneCare managed, total cost of care plus population-health programs, reaches about 22%. I hold to the strict definition, for the reason the next paragraphs give: reconciled money leaves the provider facing fee-for-service economics at the margin, which is the thing the model was for. But a reader is entitled to the other two rungs, and to the fact that the headline is the smallest of them.

And the strict series is not as monotone as three tidy rows suggest. The broader prospective share — Medicare's reconciled payment included — ran near 68% in FY18, collapsed to 31–34% across FY21–23 as hospitals exited the Medicare arrangement and COVID narrowed everything, and rebuilt only to 44% in FY24 and 41% in FY25, never regaining two-thirds of where it started. That is a different shape from the rising line above: mechanism reach fell for most of the model's life and recovered late. It does not change the strict number the marquee claim rests on, and it does not rescue the model — a measure that ends well below where it began is not a success story either. But "doubled over five years" is the strict measure's shape, not the model's, and the two should not be confused.

The arithmetic underneath the last row is not subtle. True unreconciled fixed prospective payment ran roughly $250–290 million in FY24, against a health-care system of about $6.4 billion; the GMCB's own budget order repeats in writing what Loner said in the hearing, that Medicaid was the only payer offering it (¶51). Medicare's version — an all-inclusive population-based payment that, on paper, is a comprehensive prospective payment in lieu of fee-for-service — reconciled back to fee-for-service at settlement, so providers ultimately bore fee-for-service economics; both commercial payers stayed fee-for-service for all eight years. But "four percent of the dollar" understates the case, because it counts cash flow, and cash flow is the weakest of the three things "prospective payment" is supposed to mean. Pull them apart:

  • Payment mechanism (how the cash moved): unreconciled prospective payment, the ~4% above.
  • Risk exposure (dollars genuinely at stake): about a fifth of the system sat under two-sided total-cost-of-care risk, but at low intensity. Hospital downside peaked at $36.4 million on about $1.4 billion of managed spend — 2.6% — and even that was blunted by narrow risk corridors (2%, briefly widened toward 3% only in the final years when the GMCB pushed for it) that capped exposure to a sliver. In 2022, Vermont's roughly $20 million Medicare shortfall against target fell outside the corridor and was simply not shared.
  • Marginal incentive (what a clinician faced at the point of care): essentially none. The prospective dollars were never transmitted to individual clinician compensation. This is why "reconciles to fee-for-service" does not mean "no incentive" in the abstract — the ordinary MSSP program saves money with zero prospective payment — but in Vermont the incentive intensity was diluted to homeopathy at every stage.

There is a circularity in the risk layer worth naming, because it dissolves what looked like the model's hardest edge. OneCare's downside risk was, mechanically, the network guaranteeing losses on spending that flowed to its own facilities: under OneCare's own policy the first $1.50 per member per month of downside was borne by attributing primary-care providers and the remainder by the risk-bearing hospitals — the same hospitals that received the spending. OneCare carried no third-party stop-loss. That is an internal contingency reserve, not risk transfer. Official reporting described the Medicare arrangement as population-based payment; whatever category label one attaches, reconciliation to fee-for-service changed the timing of cash, not the incentive at the point of care.

This is why the spending and utilization results should be read as too small to convict rather than as a verdict on global payment as such. You cannot indict prospective payment on Vermont's experience, because Vermont never really ran prospective payment at scale. Nor can you credit it. The model that was argued about in the newspapers and the model that touched the money were different sizes.

What actually moved the money: prices

If the object was to protect Vermonters from health-care costs, the lever that mattered was commercial prices, and the model never touched it. It ended with hospital operating finances among the worst in the region and commercial prices among the highest in the Northeast.

The single-year story people tell about 2022 — Vermont's aggregate total margin cratering to −6.6% — is true but narrow, and mostly not about operations. Massachusetts went negative that year too (−1.7%), and the whole region took the same air-pocket: the 2020–21 spike from COVID Provider Relief Fund payments and a strong investment market unwound into a 2022 bond-and-equity bear market. Decompose Vermont's 13-point total-margin fall from 2021 to 2022 and its operating margin barely moved — essentially all of the swing was the collapse of non-operating income.

What survives is the within-state trajectory: Vermont's operating margin did not recover across the model's back half while its total margin swung on investment returns. An earlier version ranked Vermont worst in the region on operating margin; that claim is withdrawn and the reasons are in the appendix. The all-payer model did not stabilize hospital finance; it coincided with the region's worst operating stretch in a decade, driven by labor and traveler costs, payer mix, and the price dynamics below.

Vermont's commercial prices ran 283% of Medicare in 2022 — above the national average and the highest in the Northeast after New York. Outpatient ran 311%, and it was still climbing: every major Vermont hospital system's outpatient price rose across 2020–22, the University of Vermont Health Network's from 329% to 357%. The GMCB's own claims study found the state and school employee plans paying nearly three times Medicare, with something like $400 million foregone against a 200% cap. The answer the state has now reached for — Act 167 reference-based pricing, arriving FY2027 — is direct price regulation, which is precisely the thing the all-payer model was built to avoid having to do.

The political economy

The version of the critique the model's defenders find easiest to answer is "it enriched a hospital network." Set that strawman aside; the real political economy is subtler and more damning, and most of it never appears in the outcome data. The state's cost-containment vehicle was a limited-liability company owned and governed by the state's dominant price-setters — the hospitals whose commercial prices the previous section shows rising. A cost-containment institution controlled by the entities whose costs it exists to contain is a governance fact worth stating plainly, and it was never on the public ledger.

The November 2022 hearing put the sharpest version of it into the record, almost in passing. Foster asked the executives directly whether they were UVM Medical Center employees. They were — "our employment attachment is UVMMC," the chief operating officer said — and UVMMC set their salaries under its own compensation policy, with the OneCare board setting only the chief executive's. OneCare was a separate limited-liability 501(c)(3) whose sole member was UVMMC. So the entity charged with holding Vermont's dominant hospital network accountable for total cost of care was staffed by that network's employees, on that network's pay scale. In the same hearing OneCare was moving staff and analytics into the network itself and contracting the services back; a board member noted what that looked like from outside — "it's UVM employees taking UVM and data services under the CFO's management to aggregate quality and operational data throughout the whole state" — and got assurances about firewalls and protections rather than a change of course. None of this was hidden, and none of it appears in any outcome measure. It is the kind of fact that only shows up if somebody reads the transcript.

Three quieter incentives held the arrangement together. First, an administrative shield: Advanced Alternative Payment Model status carried a Medicare exemption from the individual MIPS reporting regime plus a 5% bonus — and while the bonus dollars get counted, the value of the avoided penalties and administrative relief does not, though it was a first-order reason for clinicians to participate. Second, regulatory forbearance: for eight years "we are doing payment reform" was the available answer to every proposal for direct rate regulation. Participation bought time against rate-setting, and the plausible counterfactual is that Vermont would have regulated prices earlier; read that way, commercial prices at 283% of Medicare and rising are the shield working as intended, and Act 167 is the tail of that argument with the body finally supplied. Third — and this one cuts for the mechanism, which is why it belongs here — fixed prospective payments were the best revenue instrument a hospital could hold in 2020: predictable cash through the pandemic's volume collapse, a genuine benefit that providers corroborated to NORC's interviewers. An honest accounting keeps that entry.

The last thread runs to consolidation. Independent practices paid into the arrangement and were attributed to it without a share of its governance; fourteen of them moved to leave in 2020 over a per-member payment cut. Dues and attribution without a vote is the political-economy face of the consolidation curve the next section reads structurally.

Consolidation: real, but regional

One durable charge against the model is that it accelerated the consolidation of independent practice into the hospital network. The direction is right and the attribution is weak. Vermont's count of physician-office establishments fell 26% from 2014 to 2023 — the worst in its region against a flat national line — and the offices that remained grew about half again larger. But the decline began before the model, and Maine fell 23% with no all-payer model at all.

The institutional history behind the curve is real: across exactly the model window, Vermont's community hospitals folded into a single network — Central Vermont Medical Center by 2011, Porter Medical Center in 2017 — while New Hampshire's equivalent move, the Dartmouth–GraniteOne merger, was blocked by the state attorney general in 2022. That is a genuine difference between the two states. But hospital-acquired practices reclassify out of the physician-office count regardless of any payment reform, and Maine consolidated without a model, so the timeline sharpens the question rather than answering it: did the model accelerate what the network roll-up would have done anyway? The clean two-state contrast — Vermont against New Hampshire, county by county — sharpens it further, and not in the direction the strongest version of the charge requires.

Chittenden itself fell 13% and Addison held flat, while Orleans, Windsor, and Rutland — counties with no network hospital — each lost between two-fifths and half of their physician offices. New Hampshire shows the same shape faintly, rural edges thinning while its population centers held, at a tenth of Vermont's statewide magnitude. What the county grain shows is rural practice attrition steepest at the margins of the state, not absorption concentrated at the network's core — one more reason to read the consolidation curve as a regional structural story the model rode along with rather than one it drove. The methodology note records what this data can and cannot resolve; entity-level attribution — who was acquired by whom, when — is beyond establishment counts entirely.

What it is not is nine figures of waste on top of the program payments: the ~$113M administrative line runs about 1% of the $1.2–1.4 billion OneCare managed, against 8–12% commercial and roughly 10% Medicaid-MCO norms, and the large "invested in primary care" numbers the operator cites are pass-through transfers to providers, not deadweight. Both statements — the administration was cheap for what it was, and it bought an effect too small to measure — are true, and the essay's discipline is to say both.

Was it at least a good federal-money strategy?

A subtler defense holds that whatever the model did clinically, it was a smart way to draw federal dollars into a small state. Before testing it, let me be clear about the register: what follows is an accounting argument about what the model was worth as a money strategy, not a causal claim about what happened at the point of care — the causal question is exactly the uncertain quantity this argument brackets. Add the channels up. Total documented extraction over the decade — the SIM grant ($45M), the APM side payments ($42M+), the net Medicare shared savings actually retained (~$20–25M, after the advances that were steered into pre-existing state programs and the quality withholds — the published gross settlements sum to about $97M but a single year, 2022, shows $9.57M gross netting to roughly $0.5M), the Advanced-APM 5% clinician bonuses ($17–29M against Vermont's ~$125M/yr Part B professional base), and the federal Medicaid match on program costs (~$15–20M) — comes to roughly $140–160 million over ten years, one to two percent of a single year of Vermont health spending. Against that, if NORC's estimate is causal, the model reduced federal claims flowing into Vermont by $186 million. ACO economics are built so that CMS keeps most of any savings; a state cannot get rich running a successful ACO.

The fork, and its interior. Causality is a continuum, not a coin flip, so the honest form of the argument is a break-even. Let f be the fraction of NORC's estimated Medicare savings that is genuinely causal. Vermont's net federal-dollar position is the extraction total E (≈$150M, roughly independent of f, since shared savings are paid against negotiated benchmarks whether or not the savings were real) minus f times the savings S (≈$186M over the measured 2018–22 window). The break-even is f* = E/S ≈ 0.75–0.85: Vermont came out ahead on federal dollars unless at least roughly three-quarters of the estimated savings were real. I cannot bound f away from that number, and I should not pretend otherwise: the statewide placebo test (p = 0.32) is a weak instrument on this outcome, so it cannot be turned around and used to cap f. NORC's own estimate is the only direct evidence on f, and it points high. What the break-even does instead is locate the question: the federal-dollar case for the model requires a large f, and a large f is precisely the world in which the clinical case is strong too. If the causal savings continued past 2022, S grows and f* falls toward ~0.5; if instead one counts the gross settlements rather than the net retained, E rises enough that f* exceeds 1 — the extraction reading then wins outright, on every value of f. The verdict is monotone across the whole interior: there is no value of f the evidence permits at which the model becomes a good federal-money play and a real clinical success. It was a good federal-money strategy only in the world where it did little at the point of care. Contrast Maryland, whose global-budget waiver guarantees its hospitals on the order of $3 billion a year in Medicare revenue above national rates (2026 reporting; older estimates put it nearer $2 billion) — an actual extraction machine, because Maryland regulates prices. Vermont's agreement contained no rate provision at all. The dollars that did arrive financed operations, per-member payments to hospital service areas, and analytics infrastructure. Point-of-care payment rates were untouched.

What it means for AHEAD

The most policy-useful way to state the verdict is not "it was a wash." It is that the experiment was built such that its success or failure was, at the state level, largely unmeasurable. Four things stack in the same direction. The dose: four cents of the dollar. The dilution: half of one payer, none of commercial. The attenuated contrast: Vermont entered 2018 with 51.6% of its Medicare fee-for-service population already in an ACO — second in the nation, and about twenty points above its own synthetic control — so 2018 marks a change of ACO regime rather than the arrival of one, and the increment being tested is smaller than the reform being argued about. And the permutation floor: with about 49 placebos the finest detectable p is roughly 0.02. Put together, the design could not have detected an effect of the size a four-percent mechanism could plausibly produce. A null from an underpowered test is not evidence of no effect; it is evidence the test could not see one.

That claim was an assertion in an earlier version of this essay, stacked out of four qualitative reasons, and a reader was entitled to ask for the number. Asking for it changed the answer, which is why the earlier specification is now recorded in an appendix rather than on this page. The test detects Vermont when its post-period deviation exceeds the placebo states'; working backwards from that distribution gives the smallest effect this design could have seen. Reaching conventional significance would have required a sustained spending gap of about $620 to $800 per beneficiary-year, against an actual gap of $394. Converted to the population the model actually reached — roughly 40% of fee-for-service beneficiaries — that is a per-attributed-life effect of roughly $1,500 to $1,900 a year, about two to two-and-a-half times the $758 the federal evaluation estimated.

So the claim has to be narrowed, and narrowing it is the honest outcome of having computed it. The design was not blind. It was short by a factor of about two — close enough that a Vermont effect at twice NORC's estimated size would have registered, and far enough that an effect at NORC's actual size could not. The earlier version of this essay put that shortfall at a factor of nineteen and concluded no design could have seen anything; that was wrong, and it was wrong in the direction that flattered the argument. What is left is narrower and still holds: this design could not resolve an effect of the magnitude anyone actually claims for the model, and the reason is not subtle — a statewide series averages a treated half against an untreated half, and a state is one unit.

What remains is a claim about the estimand rather than the estimator, and it is the more durable version. Better specification bought a factor of nine here, and a redesign buys more. Running the obvious one — a county-level dose-response across 2,702 counties in 50 states, regressing the change in Medicare spending on the change in ACO penetration with state fixed effects and errors clustered by state — resolves effects roughly eight times smaller than the statewide design can, and returns a precisely estimated zero: $0.24 per percentage point of penetration, standard error $0.76. Over the 50-point penetration spread that separates a saturated county from an unpenetrated one, that is $12 per beneficiary-year, against a detectable floor of about $105. A pre-trend placebo on the same specification is null, and counting the suppressed small cells at their upper bound rather than zero barely moves the coefficient. NORC's per-attributed-life estimate would imply about $7.58 per point; this design rules that out by roughly ten standard errors. Nationally, across every county CMS reports, accountable-care penetration does not move Medicare spending by anything close to the amount claimed for it. But that design answers a different question. It would measure what accountable-care penetration does in general; it cannot isolate what Vermont's all-payer wrapper did, because the wrapper was a state-level policy with one unit, and no amount of geographic resolution manufactures a second Vermont. The limit is not the analyst's arithmetic. It is that the thing being evaluated was implemented once, at a scale where the only clean comparison is to a Vermont that does not exist. (One note on the calculation: it assumes a constant post-period gap, the friendliest available assumption — a gap that grows over time would have to be larger still at its peak.)

It bears directly on what came next, because the successor is not a continuation. AHEAD is a sunset, not a cancellation for failure — the agreement expired on schedule — and its design is itself evidence for the reading here: AHEAD keeps hospital global budgets and drops the statewide-ACO wrapper, the part of the Vermont model that this essay finds touched four cents of the dollar. Pennsylvania's Rural Health Model ran the same wager on a smaller stage (hospital global budgets, 2019–2024) and reached a compatible place: it stabilized participating hospitals' finances but produced no significant community-level drop in avoidable utilization, and it ended in December 2024. The through-line is Maryland's: Maryland extracts and contains because it regulates prices, Vermont governed everything except price, and Act 167 plus AHEAD are Vermont's convergence, eight years late, on the mechanism Maryland used all along.

One gap has to be named because it is the central one. The only payer that ever ran true prospective payment — Medicaid, the four cents — has no causal outcome analysis at all: NORC could not produce a Medicaid estimate for data reasons, and Vermont's all-payer claims database (VHCURES) is effectively inaccessible to independent analysts, which is itself part of the transparency critique. By the essay's own logic, the one place the mechanism actually operated is the one place no one can evaluate it. That is not a hole in this retrospective so much as a hole in the record it had to work from.

What the wind-down will test

The model ended, which means it left behind a second experiment: what happens when it is removed. I have written down six predictions — spending reversion, the effects of losing the payment waivers, primary-care contact, practice attrition, the ED trajectory, and reporting burden — and frozen them, pre-registered, before any 2026 outcome data existed, so that the retrospective cannot quietly rewrite itself to fit whatever arrives. The primary-care prediction now carries an explicit third branch — Blueprint for Health as the rival driver — so that a favorable reading is not credited to the model by default. There is a real constraint on the window: Vermont's AHEAD hospital global budgets begin in January 2027, which makes 2026 the only clean post-model year the data will ever offer. The review dates are the second quarter of 2027, when practice and reporting data land, and the second quarter of 2028, when the claims data catches up.

Until then, the accounting stands where the data leaves it. Vermont did not transform its health care and did not get robbed. It ran a careful, costly experiment on four percent of its health-care dollar, held the rest of the system constant, and cannot say what that four percent did — not because nobody looked, but because the experiment was built at a scale its own evaluation could not resolve. That is the finding. It is a smaller and stranger thing than either side wanted, and it is worth knowing.

Which leaves the standard Thom Walsh set in that hearing room in 2022, and never got met: tables and charts of where things were at the beginning, what interventions were used, and what the performance is now. It was not an unreasonable thing to ask of an organization eight years and nine figures into the work — and it is worth being clear that the answer, honestly assembled, would have been mostly negative space. Not "here is what we achieved," but "here is what we can and cannot resolve, and why." Everything above is an outsider's attempt at that, four years late, built from public files by someone with no access to the claims data the model itself ran on. What I could not do from outside is exactly the point: the one payer that ran the mechanism has no causal analysis, and the claims database that might have supplied one is closed. The accounting anyone could do was always going to end here. The accounting that would have settled it was never going to come from outside — and it never came from inside either.
