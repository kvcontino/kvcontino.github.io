--- layout: default title: "OneCare Vermont: An Accounting" description: "What the public data says Vermont's eight-year all-payer ACO experiment did — and did not — do." ---

For eight years Vermont ran the most ambitious all-payer payment experiment in the country, moving more than a billion dollars a year in provider payments through a single accountable care organization. It sunset at the end of 2025. This is an accounting of what the public data says it did — and did not — do.

By Kevin Contino · July 2026 · Every figure below is rebuilt from public data; the code, the source files, and a methodology note are linked throughout and collected at the end.

Two claims have competed over Vermont's all-payer model since it ended. One is that it was a transformation — that global payment bent the cost curve, moved care upstream, and left the system healthier. The other is that it was a boondoggle — an expensive administrative layer that enriched a hospital network and delivered nothing. The public data supports neither. What it shows is a wash: spending and outcomes that moved, at most, a little, and mostly in ways that do not survive a placebo test — with one exception that runs the wrong way for the model's own theory of itself.

That is a deflationary reading, and I want to be precise about what it does and does not say. It does not say the people who built and ran OneCare were foolish or idle; the administration was real, competent, and in its late years carried out under genuine strain. It does not say nothing good happened — the federal evaluation found rising primary-care contact, and that shows up in the data below. It says the mechanism the model is named for barely touched the Vermont health-care dollar, and that where the model's own theory made a testable promise, the strongest signal in the data points the other way. I built the case back up from primary sources so that the claim rests on something an adversary could check. The methodology note and the data are there for exactly that.

The spending question

The model's central financial promise was lower total cost of care. To test it without a single comparison state doing all the work, I built a synthetic Vermont — a weighted blend of other states, fit to match Vermont's pre-model Medicare spending trajectory, then carried forward as the counterfactual. The gap between real and synthetic Vermont after 2018 is the model's apparent effect.

So the savings replicate in direction and not in significance. Vermont did spend less than its counterfactual; so did a great many states that were running no such experiment. This is the first and most important place where the transformation reading thins out: the headline number is real enough to report and too fragile to lean on. The federal evaluator, NORC, reached a compatible place by a different route, estimating reduced Medicare claims but hedging causation heavily. I take the honest summary to be: a small favorable spending signal, indistinguishable from noise at the state level.

The one alarming exception

Total spending is a blunt instrument. The theory underneath it is specific: an ACO paid to keep a population healthy should move care upstream — fewer emergency-department visits, fewer avoidable admissions, more primary care. That is a directional prediction, and it is checkable against the same synthetic control. Put spending, inpatient stays, and emergency-department visits on one scale and the picture is not ambiguous.

This is the exception that keeps the reading from being a simple shrug. Of the three outcomes, the one that moved most is the one the model was supposed to move down, and it moved up, and it kept moving up through the mature years. I do not claim the model caused Vermonters to visit emergency rooms more; the rural Northeast has its own reasons, and the placebo p is 0.18, not 0.02. I claim something narrower and harder to wave off: in the single place where global payment made a falsifiable promise about the point of care, the data broke against it. A theory earns credit for its confirmed predictions and pays for its disconfirmed ones. This one is in arrears.

Population health: the targets met themselves

Vermont's model was reported, year after year, as meeting its population-health targets. Both halves of that sentence are true and only one is informative, because the targets were set against Vermont's own projected baseline rather than against what comparable states actually did. Measure Vermont against its New England peers instead, and the flagship targets — overdose and suicide mortality — deteriorated faster than the comparators across the model window.

Two fairness notes, because they cut in opposite directions and both belong here. First: no one credible thinks an accountable care organization could have stopped a fentanyl wave, and the overdose curve is a fentanyl curve. But that cuts against counting the target regime as evidence of success just as much as it cuts against blaming the model for the deaths — if the ACO cannot be charged with the mortality, it cannot be credited with meeting a target defined around it. Second, and in the model's favor: the chronic-disease and access measures from BRFSS show no divergence from peers at all — nothing got visibly worse — and the one genuinely favorable comparative signal is that Vermont's share of adults reporting a personal doctor pulled further ahead of peers after 2021, consistent with NORC's finding of rising primary-care contact. That is a real result and I want it on the ledger.

What the data will not support is the strongest version of the failure claim either. The figure of "20,000 premature deaths" that has attached itself to the Vermont experiment fails arithmetic outright: Vermont's all-cause mortality runs about 6% above its regional peers and below the national rate, and 20,000 excess deaths over eight years would be roughly a third of all deaths in the state, every year, unmissable in any dataset. The population-health case for the model is empty; the population-health case against it is overstated. Both statements are the same kind of statement — a refusal to read a number as more than it is.

Four cents on the dollar

Here is the finding that reframes all the others. The model's premise — providers freed from fee-for-service, paid prospectively to manage a population — describes about four percent of Vermont health-care spending at its peak. Everything above is measured against a mechanism that, in dollar terms, barely existed.

The arithmetic is not subtle. True unreconciled fixed prospective payment ran roughly $250–290 million in FY24, against a health-care system of about $6.4 billion. The GMCB's own budget order states plainly that Medicaid was the only payer offering it (¶51). Medicare's version reconciled back to fee-for-service at settlement; both commercial payers stayed fee-for-service for all eight years. Hospital downside "risk" — the part that was supposed to concentrate the mind — peaked at $36.4 million on about $1.4 billion of managed spend, 2.6%, roughly one year of OneCare's own operating and program budget. The engine was connected to four percent of the drivetrain, and to none of the commercial half of the system at all.

This is why the spending and utilization results should be read as a wash rather than a verdict on global payment as such. You cannot indict prospective payment on Vermont's experience, because Vermont never really ran prospective payment at scale. Nor can you credit it. The model that was argued about in the newspapers and the model that touched the money were different sizes.

What actually moved the money: prices

If the object was to protect Vermonters from health-care costs, the lever that mattered was commercial prices, and the model never touched it. It ended with hospital finances worse than the region's and commercial prices among the highest in New England.

Vermont hospitals entered the model roughly at the regional norm and left it the worst performers in the neighborhood, bottoming at a −6.6% aggregate total margin in 2022 while New Hampshire's climbed. Global budgeting did not stabilize hospital finance; it coincided with its worst stretch in a decade. And the cost-shift that global payment was supposed to make unnecessary went the other way. Vermont's commercial prices ran 283% of Medicare in 2022 — above the national average and the highest in New England after New York. Outpatient ran 311%, and the University of Vermont Health Network's outpatient price climbed from 329% to 357% of Medicare across 2020–22, the model's mature years. The GMCB's own claims study found the state and school employee plans paying nearly three times Medicare, with something like $400 million foregone against a 200% cap. The answer the state has now reached for — Act 167 reference-based pricing, arriving FY2027 — is direct price regulation, which is precisely the thing the all-payer model was built to avoid having to do.

Consolidation: real, but regional

One durable charge against the model is that it accelerated the consolidation of independent practice into the hospital network. The direction is right and the attribution is weak. Vermont's count of physician-office establishments fell 26% from 2014 to 2023 — the worst in its region against a flat national line — and the offices that remained grew about half again larger. But the decline began before the model, and Maine fell 23% with no all-payer model at all.

The institutional history behind the curve is real: across exactly the model window, Vermont's community hospitals folded into a single network — Central Vermont Medical Center by 2011, Porter Medical Center in 2017 — while New Hampshire's equivalent move, the Dartmouth–GraniteOne merger, was blocked by the state attorney general in 2022. That is a genuine difference between the two states. But hospital-acquired practices reclassify out of the physician-office count regardless of any payment reform, and Maine consolidated without a model, so the timeline sharpens the question rather than answering it: did the model accelerate what the network roll-up would have done anyway? The clean two-state contrast — Vermont against New Hampshire, county by county — is the next cut worth making. The methodology note records how far the data currently reaches and where it stops.

The ledger

Set the certain costs against the uncertain savings and the whole thing lands near break-even — resting, like everything else, on an estimate that does not survive a placebo test.

Nine figures of certain, visible administration against a savings estimate that does not clear a state-level placebo test. That is the ledger. It is nowhere near the transformation that was promised and nowhere near the boondoggle that has been alleged. It is an expensive, competently run experiment whose measurable effect at the point of care rounds to zero, with one utilization signal pointing the wrong way and one access signal pointing the right way.

Was it at least a good way to capture federal money?

A subtler defense holds that whatever the model did clinically, it was a smart way to draw federal dollars into a small state. Add the channels up and the defense inverts on itself. Total documented extraction over the decade — the SIM grant ($45M), the APM side payments ($42M+), Medicare shared savings (~$20–25M), the Advanced-APM 5% clinician bonuses (an estimated $17–29M, small money against Vermont's ~$125M/yr Part B professional base), and the federal Medicaid match on program costs (~$15–20M) — comes to roughly $140–160 million over ten years, one to two percent of a single year of Vermont health spending. Against that, if NORC's estimate is causal, the model reduced federal claims flowing into Vermont by $186 million. ACO economics are built so that CMS keeps most of any savings; a state cannot get rich running a successful ACO.

The fork. If the savings were real, the model was a net-negative federal-money play — it gave back more in reduced claims than it drew in grants and bonuses. If the savings were not real, which the placebo p = 0.64 permits, then Vermont kept normal Medicare revenue and collected the side payments and bonuses — a modestly successful money chase, precisely to the degree the model changed nothing at the point of care. Either branch supports the same deflationary reading: it was a good federal-money strategy only in the world where it did nothing. Contrast Maryland, whose global-budget waiver guarantees its hospitals something like $2 billion a year above federal rates — an actual extraction machine. Vermont's agreement contained no rate provision at all. The dollars that did arrive financed operations, per-member payments to hospital service areas, and analytics infrastructure. Point-of-care payment rates were untouched.

What the wind-down will test

The model ended, which means it left behind a second experiment: what happens when it is removed. I have written down six predictions — spending reversion, the effects of losing the payment waivers, primary-care contact, practice attrition, the ED trajectory, and reporting burden — and frozen them, pre-registered, before any 2026 outcome data existed, so that the retrospective cannot quietly rewrite itself to fit whatever arrives. There is a real constraint on the window: Vermont's AHEAD hospital global budgets begin in January 2027, which makes 2026 the only clean post-model year the data will ever offer. The review dates are the second quarter of 2027, when practice and reporting data land, and the second quarter of 2028, when the claims data catches up.

Until then, the accounting stands where the data leaves it. Vermont did not transform its health care and did not get robbed. It ran a careful, costly experiment on four percent of its health-care dollar, held the rest of the system constant, and learned — at real expense, and worth knowing — how little that four percent could move on its own.
