--- layout: default title: "OneCare Vermont: An Accounting" description: "What the public data says Vermont's eight-year all-payer ACO experiment did — and did not — do." ---

For eight years Vermont ran the most ambitious all-payer payment experiment in the country, moving more than a billion dollars a year in provider payments through a single accountable care organization — though, as this accounting will show, only about four cents of each of those dollars ever changed how a provider was actually paid. It sunset at the end of 2025. This is an accounting of what the public data says it did — and did not — do.

By Kevin Contino · July 2026 · Every figure below is rebuilt from public data; the code, the source files, and a methodology note are linked throughout and collected at the end.

Two claims have competed over Vermont's all-payer model since it ended. One is that it was a transformation — that global payment bent the cost curve, moved care upstream, and left the system healthier. The other is that it was a boondoggle — an expensive administrative layer that enriched a hospital network and delivered nothing. The public data supports neither. What it shows is a model whose signature mechanism reached about four percent of the health-care dollar, and whose measurable effects at the point of care — a fragile spending signal, flat admissions, an ED trend that ran the wrong way for the model's own theory — are too small, and too diluted, to separate from ordinary noise at the state level.

That is a deflationary reading, and I want to be precise about what it does and does not say. It does not say the people who built and ran OneCare were foolish or idle; the administration was real, competent, and in its late years carried out under genuine strain. It does not say nothing good happened — the federal evaluation found rising primary-care contact, and that shows up in the data below. It says the mechanism the model is named for barely touched the Vermont health-care dollar, and that the design was built such that whether it worked or failed was, at the state level, largely unmeasurable. I built the case back up from primary sources so that the claim rests on something an adversary could check. The methodology note and the data are there for exactly that.

The spending question

The model's central financial promise was lower total cost of care. To test it without a single comparison state doing all the work, I built a synthetic Vermont — a weighted blend of other states, fit to match Vermont's pre-model Medicare spending trajectory, then carried forward as the counterfactual. The gap between real and synthetic Vermont after 2018 is the model's apparent effect. One thing to hold onto about what this estimand can mean: Vermont was already a mature ACO state before 2018, and the donor states are ordinary ACO-saturated Medicare. So the number below is not "the model versus no reform" — it is the incremental effect of the all-payer wrapper over a baseline that already contained garden-variety Medicare ACO participation.

The savings replicate in direction and not in significance, and they are fragile in three separate ways. The pre-period fit is the worst of the three outcomes — Vermont sits near the bottom of the national spending distribution and no donor blend fully reaches it, so the fit is off by about 4% of the level even before treatment. The point estimate is a two-state tug-of-war: dropping either of the two dominant donors swings the 2024 gap from −$567 to −$1,111. And the direction, though real, does not clear a placebo test. Two of those three are the kind of thing a hostile analyst would seize on, so I am flagging them myself.

There is a fourth problem, and it cuts the other way from how it first reads. Netted against the pre-period, the −$462 gap is roughly 4–5% of Medicare spending — double or triple the net savings that mature MSSP ACOs post (McWilliams and colleagues put those at 1–2%), from a far more dilute intervention that reached only part of one payer. The most on-point Medicare benchmark, the Next Generation ACO model, found no net savings at all after side payments and was discontinued by CMS at the end of 2021. A savings signal several times larger than mature programs produce, from a mechanism several times weaker, is not a triumph to bank; it is prima facie evidence that the estimate is catching something other than the model — most of it, as the utilization section will show, arriving after 2020. The honest summary is a small, favorable, and untrustworthy spending signal.

The utilization promise

Total spending is a blunt instrument. The theory underneath it is specific: an ACO paid to keep a population healthy should move care upstream — fewer emergency-department visits, fewer avoidable admissions, more primary care. That is a directional prediction, and it is checkable against the same synthetic control. Put spending, inpatient stays, and emergency-department visits on one scale and the ED series is the one that moved.

This is the finding a reader is most tempted to treat as a verdict, so it is worth being exact about what standard it meets and what it does not. The model neither prevented the ED rise nor plausibly caused it, and at four percent penetration it was not positioned to do either. The signal is real enough to report — it is the highest-ranked of the three outcomes against placebos — but the placebo p is 0.18, not 0.02, which on 50 states means Vermont is roughly the ninth-most-extreme, not an outlier. And three things keep it from indicting the model:

The federal evaluation points the same way, not the other. NORC's own beneficiary-level measure for the attributed Medicare population — ED visits and observation stays combined — also rose, by 40.7 per 1,000 (about 8% above baseline), non-significant (p = 0.32), directionally consistent with the statewide climb here. The one utilization measure NORC found significant was a fall in acute inpatient stays (−35.6 per 1,000, p < 0.10) — and part of that decline is deliberate substitution of observation stays for admissions, which lands inside the ED-and-observation measure. So flat inpatient counts alongside a rising ED-and-observation count is partly the admission-to-observation signature that ACO theory predicts as a success. The testable promise the data can see (all ED visits) is coarser than the promise the theory makes (avoidable ED visits); the coarser measure cannot cleanly separate the two. This is the closest the attributed-population evaluation comes to the statewide finding, and it corroborates rather than contradicts it.

Most of the gap is post-2020, and there is a live rival for it. A modest gap was already visible in 2018–19 (about +29 per 1,000), but the divergence roughly doubles after 2020 and the post-2020 years account for 86% of the cumulative total — squarely the window of the national and Vermont-specific ED-boarding and workforce crisis, which NORC names as its own explanation. There is also a denominator problem the synthetic control cannot reach. The spending and ED series are Medicare fee-for-service only, and Vermont's FFS denominator shrank 16.5% from its 2019 peak as Medicare Advantage grew — the UVM Health Network co-launched a Medicare Advantage plan (UVM Health Advantage, with MVP) in January 2022, and Vermont's MA penetration crossed above its synthetic comparator's in exactly that year. If healthier beneficiaries are the ones leaving FFS, the shrinking denominator mechanically inflates per-capita FFS spending and ED rates with no change in real utilization. Robustness checks that match MA penetration in the pre-period move the estimate by less than 2% — but by construction they cannot reach a divergence that opens up entirely after 2021, which is exactly where the reviewers said to expect it to hide.

This was the best of three outcomes. Three outcomes were tested; under the global null, one p around 0.18 in three tries is unremarkable, and the section is on the one that came out most extreme. Unlike the pre-registered wind-down test below, this retrospective synthetic control was not pre-registered. So the honest claim is narrow: in the one place the model made a falsifiable promise about the point of care, the data did not move in its favor, and it did not move enough against it to convict. The resolution is pre-registered as hypothesis H5 — if the +18% gap was model-caused, removing the model should bend it; continued growth on trend exonerates the model on this margin.

Who the model actually reached

Any reader who has worked with ACOs will look first for the number the essay above has been circling: how many Vermonters were actually attributed to OneCare, and how much of the state did that leave out. It matters because a real per-attributed-life effect is diluted two to three times over before it reaches a statewide Medicare series — which is both why the synthetic control could miss a genuine effect and why the effect it does show should be read as smaller than the model's own internal estimates.

Attribution grew from about 110,000 lives (22% of eligible) in 2018 to a peak of 259,958 (50%) in 2022, then went backwards: Blue Cross Blue Shield of Vermont withdrew for 2023, erasing roughly 93,000 lives — about a third of the portfolio — so the commercial "all-payer" leg effectively collapsed before the model's scheduled end. Even the payer with real prospective payment reached only about half its own population: in 2020, OneCare's 49,337 attributed Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries were 57.5% of the 85,792 living in-state — and that FFS denominator was itself shrinking as Medicare Advantage grew. The churn was not only at the payer level; fourteen independent primary-care practices moved to quit in August 2020 over a cut to the per-member payment, resolved only by preserving the payment through 2021.

Put the dilution in one line. A real effect concentrated in the attributed population — roughly half of Medicare, none of commercial in payment terms — is averaged across a statewide series in which the other half moves on its own. The federal evaluation's estimand (attributed lives, matched to out-of-state ACOs) and this essay's (all Vermont fee-for-service) are therefore not interchangeable: dilution predicts the statewide estimate should be the smaller of the two, and it is. This is the arithmetic underneath the four-cents finding two sections down.

The model's own scorecard

Before grading the model against an outside counterfactual, it is worth scoring it against its own contract — because the state's official scoreboard reaches a version of the same conclusion, and citing it converts "my strict definition" into "the model's own numbers." The All-Payer Model Agreement set three kinds of target.

On the Medicare growth target — per-beneficiary total cost of care growing 0.2 points below projected national Medicare growth — the model met the target every one of its five performance years (NORC, Fourth Report, p.26; in PY5 Vermont ran 6.6 to 8.4 points below its Medicare targets). That is the model's own scorecard recording a genuine hit, and it belongs on the page. On the all-payer growth target — 3.5% compound annual growth, the binding metric assessed at model end — the annual figures that are public are mixed (2018 came in at 4.1%, within the 4.3% enforcement threshold; 2019 near 4.6%, above the 3.5% goal), and the final compound verdict was not confirmed to a single citable GMCB statement in this pass, so I state only what is documented and no more. On the scale targets — the share of Vermonters and of Medicare beneficiaries the model had to enroll — it fell short throughout, and CMS waived enforcement in October 2021, deeming the targets unattainable.

That scale failure is the state's own official version of the four-cents finding: by the government's own scoreboard the model never reached the reach it was built to reach. What the scoreboard cannot do is credit the growth hit as an effect. The growth targets were set against Vermont's own projected baseline, not against what comparable states actually did, and with no comparator a target beaten is a projection beaten — which is why the synthetic control, not the scorecard, carries the causal question.

Population health: the targets met themselves

Vermont's model was reported, year after year, as meeting its population-health targets. Both halves of that sentence are true and only one is informative, because the targets were set against Vermont's own projected baseline rather than against what comparable states actually did. Measure Vermont against its Northeast peers instead, and the story on the two flagship causes — overdose and suicide — is not the deterioration the failure case wants, nor the success the target regime claims. It is mostly a pre-existing gap the model did not touch.

Take the two causes in turn, because they fail the model's target regime in different ways. Suicide is a level difference, not a trend: Vermont has run several points per 100,000 above its peers since around 2010 — eight years before the model — and the gap did not widen during the model window (+5.2 immediately before, +5.5 during, age-adjusted, well inside the year-to-year noise). A level that predates the model cannot indict it; by the same token, a suicide "target" scored against Vermont's own baseline cannot credit it. Overdose is a genuine deterioration relative to peers — the multi-year climb survives the Poisson noise in the counts — but the overdose curve is a fentanyl curve, and no one credible thinks an accountable care organization could have stopped a fentanyl wave. That cuts against counting the target regime as success exactly as much as it cuts against blaming the model for the deaths. Both flagship targets, in other words, are scored on quantities the model neither moved nor was built to move.

Two further notes belong here. The chronic-disease and access measures from BRFSS show no divergence from peers at all — nothing got visibly worse — and the one genuinely favorable comparative signal is that Vermont's share of adults reporting a personal doctor pulled further ahead of peers after 2021, consistent with NORC's finding of rising primary-care contact. I want that on the ledger, with two caveats stated in the same breath: the personal-doctor question was redesigned in the 2021 BRFSS cycle (PERSDOC2 to PERSDOC3), so the comparison is of gaps, not levels; and Vermont's Blueprint for Health has been paying for primary-care medical homes for a decade, which confounds the primary-care signal with the model at least as plausibly as it credits it. That is why Blueprint enters the pre-registered hypotheses below as a rival explanation, not a footnote.

What the data will not support is the strongest version of the failure claim either. The figure of "20,000 premature deaths" that has attached itself to the Vermont experiment fails arithmetic outright: Vermont's all-cause mortality runs about 6% above its regional peers on an age-adjusted basis (VSRR, 2023: 740.8 vs. a peer mean of 698.5 per 100,000) and below the national rate — the crude excess a second-oldest state would show anyway is not the point — and 20,000 excess deaths over eight years would be roughly a third of all deaths in the state, every year, unmissable in any dataset. One more caution belongs on every panel here: mortality and chronic disease respond to delivery-system change on a 5-to-15-year lag, so the utilization window is adequate to the model but the mortality window is not, whatever the design. The population-health case for the model is empty; the population-health case against it is overstated. Both statements are the same kind of statement — a refusal to read a number as more than it is.

Four cents on the dollar

Here is the finding that reframes all the others. The model's premise — providers freed from fee-for-service, paid prospectively to manage a population — describes about four percent of Vermont health-care spending at its peak. Everything above is measured against a mechanism that, in dollar terms, barely existed.

The arithmetic is not subtle. True unreconciled fixed prospective payment ran roughly $250–290 million in FY24, against a health-care system of about $6.4 billion. The GMCB's own budget order states plainly that Medicaid was the only payer offering it (¶51). Medicare's version — an all-inclusive population-based payment that, on paper, is a comprehensive prospective payment in lieu of fee-for-service — reconciled back to fee-for-service at settlement, so providers ultimately bore fee-for-service economics; both commercial payers stayed fee-for-service for all eight years. But "four percent of the dollar" understates the case, because it counts cash flow, and cash flow is the weakest of the three things "prospective payment" is supposed to mean. Pull them apart:

  • Payment mechanism (how the cash moved): unreconciled prospective payment, the ~4% above.
  • Risk exposure (dollars genuinely at stake): about a fifth of the system sat under two-sided total-cost-of-care risk, but at low intensity. Hospital downside peaked at $36.4 million on about $1.4 billion of managed spend — 2.6% — and even that was blunted by narrow risk corridors (2%, briefly widened toward 3% only in the final years when the GMCB pushed for it) that capped exposure to a sliver. In 2022, Vermont's roughly $20 million Medicare shortfall against target fell outside the corridor and was simply not shared.
  • Marginal incentive (what a clinician faced at the point of care): essentially none. The prospective dollars were never transmitted to individual clinician compensation. This is why "reconciles to fee-for-service" does not mean "no incentive" in the abstract — the ordinary MSSP program saves money with zero prospective payment — but in Vermont the incentive intensity was diluted to homeopathy at every stage.

There is a circularity in the risk layer worth naming, because it dissolves what looked like the model's hardest edge. OneCare's downside risk was, mechanically, the network guaranteeing losses on spending that flowed to its own facilities: under OneCare's own policy the first $1.50 per member per month of downside was borne by attributing primary-care providers and the remainder by the risk-bearing hospitals — the same hospitals that received the spending. OneCare carried no third-party stop-loss. That is an internal contingency reserve, not risk transfer. Official reporting described the Medicare arrangement as population-based payment; whatever category label one attaches, reconciliation to fee-for-service changed the timing of cash, not the incentive at the point of care.

This is why the spending and utilization results should be read as too small to convict rather than as a verdict on global payment as such. You cannot indict prospective payment on Vermont's experience, because Vermont never really ran prospective payment at scale. Nor can you credit it. The model that was argued about in the newspapers and the model that touched the money were different sizes.

What actually moved the money: prices

If the object was to protect Vermonters from health-care costs, the lever that mattered was commercial prices, and the model never touched it. It ended with hospital operating finances among the worst in the region and commercial prices among the highest in the Northeast.

The single-year story people tell about 2022 — Vermont's aggregate total margin cratering to −6.6% — is true but narrow, and mostly not about operations. Massachusetts went negative that year too (−1.7%), and the whole region took the same air-pocket: the 2020–21 spike from COVID Provider Relief Fund payments and a strong investment market unwound into a 2022 bond-and-equity bear market. Decompose Vermont's 13-point total-margin fall from 2021 to 2022 and its operating margin barely moved — essentially all of the swing was the collapse of non-operating income. The durable claim is the operating one, and it is worse for the model, not better: on operating margin Vermont ranked worst of its four regional peers in five of the model's last six years, not just in 2022. Its hospitals' underlying losses on patient care were the deepest in the neighborhood through most of the model's back half — a claim I attach the HCRIS caveat to every time, pending GMCB audited financials. The all-payer model did not stabilize hospital finance; it coincided with the region's worst operating stretch in a decade, driven by labor and traveler costs, payer mix, and the price dynamics below. (The essay's earlier draft called this a failure of "global budgeting" — a category error the four-cents section corrects: Vermont never ran global budgets under this model. Those arrive with AHEAD in 2027.)

Vermont's commercial prices ran 283% of Medicare in 2022 — above the national average and the highest in the Northeast after New York. Outpatient ran 311%, and it was still climbing: every major Vermont hospital system's outpatient price rose across 2020–22, the University of Vermont Health Network's from 329% to 357%. The GMCB's own claims study found the state and school employee plans paying nearly three times Medicare, with something like $400 million foregone against a 200% cap. The answer the state has now reached for — Act 167 reference-based pricing, arriving FY2027 — is direct price regulation, which is precisely the thing the all-payer model was built to avoid having to do.

The political economy

The version of the critique the model's defenders find easiest to answer is "it enriched a hospital network." Set that strawman aside; the real political economy is subtler and more damning, and most of it never appears in the outcome data. The state's cost-containment vehicle was a limited-liability company owned and governed by the state's dominant price-setters — the hospitals whose commercial prices the previous section shows rising. A cost-containment institution controlled by the entities whose costs it exists to contain is a governance fact worth stating plainly, and it was never on the public ledger.

Three quieter incentives held the arrangement together. First, an administrative shield: Advanced Alternative Payment Model status carried a Medicare exemption from the individual MIPS reporting regime plus a 5% bonus — and while the bonus dollars get counted, the value of the avoided penalties and administrative relief does not, though it was a first-order reason for clinicians to participate. Second, regulatory forbearance: for eight years "we are doing payment reform" was the available answer to every proposal for direct rate regulation. Participation bought time against rate-setting, and the plausible counterfactual is that Vermont would have regulated prices earlier; read that way, commercial prices at 283% of Medicare and rising are the shield working as intended, and Act 167 is the tail of that argument with the body finally supplied. Third — and this one cuts for the mechanism, which is why it belongs here — fixed prospective payments were the best revenue instrument a hospital could hold in 2020: predictable cash through the pandemic's volume collapse, a genuine benefit that providers corroborated to NORC's interviewers. An honest accounting keeps that entry.

The last thread runs to consolidation. Independent practices paid into the arrangement and were attributed to it without a share of its governance; fourteen of them moved to leave in 2020 over a per-member payment cut. Dues and attribution without a vote is the political-economy face of the consolidation curve the next section reads structurally.

Consolidation: real, but regional

One durable charge against the model is that it accelerated the consolidation of independent practice into the hospital network. The direction is right and the attribution is weak. Vermont's count of physician-office establishments fell 26% from 2014 to 2023 — the worst in its region against a flat national line — and the offices that remained grew about half again larger. But the decline began before the model, and Maine fell 23% with no all-payer model at all.

The institutional history behind the curve is real: across exactly the model window, Vermont's community hospitals folded into a single network — Central Vermont Medical Center by 2011, Porter Medical Center in 2017 — while New Hampshire's equivalent move, the Dartmouth–GraniteOne merger, was blocked by the state attorney general in 2022. That is a genuine difference between the two states. But hospital-acquired practices reclassify out of the physician-office count regardless of any payment reform, and Maine consolidated without a model, so the timeline sharpens the question rather than answering it: did the model accelerate what the network roll-up would have done anyway? The clean two-state contrast — Vermont against New Hampshire, county by county — sharpens it further, and not in the direction the strongest version of the charge requires.

Chittenden itself fell 13% and Addison held flat, while Orleans, Windsor, and Rutland — counties with no network hospital — each lost between two-fifths and half of their physician offices. New Hampshire shows the same shape faintly, rural edges thinning while its population centers held, at a tenth of Vermont's statewide magnitude. What the county grain shows is rural practice attrition steepest at the margins of the state, not absorption concentrated at the network's core — one more reason to read the consolidation curve as a regional structural story the model rode along with rather than one it drove. The methodology note records what this data can and cannot resolve; entity-level attribution — who was acquired by whom, when — is beyond establishment counts entirely.

The Medicaid taxpayer frame is negative in every world: the +$25.6M does not depend on the contested Medicare estimate. The societal frame is a fork: in the world where NORC's savings are causal, the balance is modestly negative-to-break-even — nine figures of certain, visible administration against a contestable $186M — and in the world where they are not, it is nine figures of administration for a point-of-care effect that rounds to zero. What it is not is nine figures of waste on top of the program payments: the ~$113M administrative line runs about 1% of the $1.2–1.4 billion OneCare managed, against 8–12% commercial and roughly 10% Medicaid-MCO norms, and the large "invested in primary care" numbers the operator cites are pass-through transfers to providers, not deadweight. Both statements — the administration was cheap for what it was, and it bought an effect too small to measure — are true, and the essay's discipline is to say both.

Was it at least a good federal-money strategy?

A subtler defense holds that whatever the model did clinically, it was a smart way to draw federal dollars into a small state. Before testing it, let me be clear about the register: what follows is an accounting argument about what the model was worth as a money strategy, not a causal claim about what happened at the point of care — the causal question is exactly the uncertain quantity this argument brackets. Add the channels up. Total documented extraction over the decade — the SIM grant ($45M), the APM side payments ($42M+), the net Medicare shared savings actually retained (~$20–25M, after the advances that were steered into pre-existing state programs and the quality withholds — the published gross settlements sum to about $97M but a single year, 2022, shows $9.57M gross netting to roughly $0.5M), the Advanced-APM 5% clinician bonuses ($17–29M against Vermont's ~$125M/yr Part B professional base), and the federal Medicaid match on program costs (~$15–20M) — comes to roughly $140–160 million over ten years, one to two percent of a single year of Vermont health spending. Against that, if NORC's estimate is causal, the model reduced federal claims flowing into Vermont by $186 million. ACO economics are built so that CMS keeps most of any savings; a state cannot get rich running a successful ACO.

The fork, and its interior. Causality is a continuum, not a coin flip, so the honest form of the argument is a break-even. Let f be the fraction of NORC's estimated Medicare savings that is genuinely causal. Vermont's net federal-dollar position is the extraction total E (≈$150M, roughly independent of f, since shared savings are paid against negotiated benchmarks whether or not the savings were real) minus f times the savings S (≈$186M over the measured 2018–22 window). The break-even is f* = E/S ≈ 0.75–0.85: Vermont came out ahead on federal dollars unless at least roughly three-quarters of the estimated savings were real — and nothing in the evidence supports a number that high, since the placebo test (p = 0.64, part of the gap predating treatment) cannot certify even the sign. If the causal savings continued past 2022, S grows and f* falls toward ~0.5; if instead one counts the gross settlements rather than the net retained, E rises enough that f* exceeds 1 — the extraction reading then wins outright, on every value of f. The verdict is monotone across the whole interior: there is no value of f the evidence permits at which the model becomes a good federal-money play and a real clinical success. It was a good federal-money strategy only in the world where it did little at the point of care. Contrast Maryland, whose global-budget waiver guarantees its hospitals on the order of $3 billion a year in Medicare revenue above national rates (2026 reporting; older estimates put it nearer $2 billion) — an actual extraction machine, because Maryland regulates prices. Vermont's agreement contained no rate provision at all. The dollars that did arrive financed operations, per-member payments to hospital service areas, and analytics infrastructure. Point-of-care payment rates were untouched.

What it means for AHEAD

The most policy-useful way to state the verdict is not "it was a wash." It is that the experiment was built such that its success or failure was, at the state level, largely unmeasurable. Take the dose (four cents of the dollar), the dilution (half of one payer, none of commercial), and the permutation floor (with about 49 placebos the finest detectable p is roughly 0.02) together, and the design could not have detected an effect of the size a four-percent mechanism could plausibly produce. A null from an underpowered test is not evidence of no effect; it is evidence the test could not see one. That is the lesson worth carrying, and it is a lesson about evaluation design as much as about payment reform.

It bears directly on what came next, because the successor is not a continuation. AHEAD is a sunset, not a cancellation for failure — the agreement expired on schedule — and its design is itself evidence for the reading here: AHEAD keeps hospital global budgets and drops the statewide-ACO wrapper, the part of the Vermont model that this essay finds touched four cents of the dollar. Pennsylvania's Rural Health Model ran the same wager on a smaller stage (hospital global budgets, 2019–2024) and reached a compatible place: it stabilized participating hospitals' finances but produced no significant community-level drop in avoidable utilization, and it ended in December 2024. The through-line is Maryland's: Maryland extracts and contains because it regulates prices, Vermont governed everything except price, and Act 167 plus AHEAD are Vermont's convergence, eight years late, on the mechanism Maryland used all along.

One gap has to be named because it is the central one. The only payer that ever ran true prospective payment — Medicaid, the four cents — has no causal outcome analysis at all: NORC could not produce a Medicaid estimate for data reasons, and Vermont's all-payer claims database (VHCURES) is effectively inaccessible to independent analysts, which is itself part of the transparency critique. By the essay's own logic, the one place the mechanism actually operated is the one place no one can evaluate it. That is not a hole in this retrospective so much as a hole in the record it had to work from.

What the wind-down will test

The model ended, which means it left behind a second experiment: what happens when it is removed. I have written down six predictions — spending reversion, the effects of losing the payment waivers, primary-care contact, practice attrition, the ED trajectory, and reporting burden — and frozen them, pre-registered, before any 2026 outcome data existed, so that the retrospective cannot quietly rewrite itself to fit whatever arrives. The primary-care prediction now carries an explicit third branch — Blueprint for Health as the rival driver — so that a favorable reading is not credited to the model by default. There is a real constraint on the window: Vermont's AHEAD hospital global budgets begin in January 2027, which makes 2026 the only clean post-model year the data will ever offer. The review dates are the second quarter of 2027, when practice and reporting data land, and the second quarter of 2028, when the claims data catches up.

Until then, the accounting stands where the data leaves it. Vermont did not transform its health care and did not get robbed. It ran a careful, costly experiment on four percent of its health-care dollar, held the rest of the system constant, and learned — at real expense, and worth knowing — how little that four percent could move on its own, and how hard the design made it to tell.
