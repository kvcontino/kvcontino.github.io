Median age is a bad way to find a young city. A garrison town, a college town, a high-fertility exurb and a genuine magnet for working-age adults all look alike on that one number, and they are entirely different places to live in or invest in. This is an attempt to tell them apart using single-year-of-age data for all 387 metropolitan statistical areas and 3,144 counties.

By Kevin Contino · July 2026 · Every figure is rebuilt from a cached source file by a make all pipeline; nothing here is quoted from memory. The full technical report and the interactive metro map carry the detail.

What the data is, and what was wrong with the plan

The source is the Census Bureau's Vintage 2025 population estimates, released June 2026, covering 4/1/2020 through 7/1/2025 — 80 files, 251 MB, each recorded with its source URL, byte size, SHA256 and retrieval timestamp. National median age on 7/1/2025 is 39.38 years, and 190 of the 387 metros sit below it.

Every single-year-of-age aggregate was reconciled against the corresponding published Census total. All residuals are exactly zero, including the one most likely to fail silently — counties aggregated up through the 2023 delineation against published CBSA totals. Median age computed by interpolation lands within 0.050 years of the published value at both CBSA and county level, which is exactly the rounding bound of a figure published to one decimal; the interpolation reproduces the Census method rather than approximating it.

Five assumptions in the original specification did not survive contact with the files. The AGE=999 and SEX=0 sentinels the plan filtered on do not exist in the metro and county files — filtering as specified would have discarded everything. Two specified paths 404. The national file runs to age 100 while the metro and county files are top-coded at 85+, which, left unaligned, injects spurious dissimilarity into every metro. Those corrections are recorded rather than quietly applied; the reconciliation table is what caught them.

Age structure has shapes, not just averages

Clustering nine features per metro — modality count, a dissimilarity index, group-quarters share by type, sex ratio at 18–29, and the under-18 and 65+ shares — reproduces some expected types and refuses to separate others. College towns, retirement destinations and aged-in-place metros each come out cleanly. High-fertility metros, border and agricultural metros, and prime-age magnets all collapse into one large cluster. That is a real result about the limits of shape-based clustering, and it is why the magnet question needs a different instrument.

Cohort progression, not net migration

The instrument is the cohort change ratio: follow one birth cohort between two dates and see whether the county still has it. CCR = P(30–34, 7/1/2025) ÷ P(25–29, 7/1/2020). Above 1.0 the county gained that cohort; below 1.0 it lost it. It is age-specific and requires no assumption about where migrants came from, which net-migration totals cannot match.

The sanity check that failed, and why that is the finding

The specification expected Austin, Raleigh, Denver, Nashville, Charlotte, Salt Lake City, Boise, Washington DC and Seattle to all land in the top quintile of a composite magnet index. Two did not. Salt Lake City–Murray reaches the 68th percentile; Washington reaches the 45th.

Verified directly against cbsa-est2025-alldata.csv: over 7/1/2020–7/1/2025 the Washington metro lost 208,058 net domestic migrants while gaining 262,675 international. Salt Lake City lost 40,598 against +51,256. Seattle (−107,029) and Denver (−33,545) show the same pattern less severely.

The index is measuring domestic attraction, and these two metros did not have it over this window. The specification's expectation encodes a pre-2020 prior. An exclusion criterion written before any of this was known independently routes both into the international-led group, which is where the specification itself says such metros belong — so the framework disagreed with its own author's expectations and turned out to be right.

Method and reproducibility

Everything above is produced by make all: fetch (cached, with a manifest), build, analyse, validate, figures, interactive map, report. The validate stage is a gate — it exits non-zero if any reconciliation check fails, so a broken join cannot reach a figure. Mapping is Albers Equal Area (EPSG:5070) with Alaska (EPSG:3338) and Hawaii (EPSG:26962) reprojected, rescaled and inset rather than silently dropped.

Known weaknesses, the group-quarters and student treatments, the argument for excluding children from the support measures, and a full data dictionary for every output table are in the technical report.
