Metro Relocation

A transparent, tunable model for screening where to live — ten U.S. metros, 118 inner-ring neighborhoods, nine dimensions, and a set of knobs that are usually hidden inside someone else's spreadsheet.

2026-07-08 · Python · GeoPandas · OSMnx · Census / OSM / FBI UCR

Most "best places to live" rankings hand you a single number and hide the value judgments that produced it. This one turns those judgments into controls. Nine dimensions describe a neighborhood — the dwelling, everyday walkability, greenery, quiet, safety, cost, distance from concentrated public housing, rail, and highway access. You decide how much each one counts and where to draw the hard lines, and the whole field re-ranks the moment you move a slider.

The default weights encode one illustrative profile: an established, quiet, inner-ring neighborhood you walk out of for daily errands and drive out of for everything else. It's a starting point, not a verdict — change it to yours.

How the scores are built

Methods

The pipeline turns raw geography into nine comparable 0–100 scores per neighborhood, then blends them. Everything the sliders do is faithful to the underlying model — the composite really is a weighted average of these nine axes.

Composite

For a neighborhood with axis scores aᵢ and your weights wᵢ (normalized to sum to 1):

composite = Σ wᵢ · aᵢ

A neighborhood is screened out if any aᵢ falls below your minimum for that axis, or if its effective rent exceeds your ceiling.

Turning geography into 0–100

Most axes are mapped from a raw metric onto 0–100 by percentile or min–max within the metro, so a score answers "how does this place compare to its peers." Two axes are deliberately saturating — a response curve that rises fast, then flattens once "enough" is reached. Everyday walkability, for instance, hits about 50 at a minimum service density and ~67 at twice that, so past a genuinely walkable threshold the axis stops rewarding ever-denser commercial cores. Rail access behaves the same way.

Safety

Violent-crime rates are put on one absolute ruler, safety = 100 · (1 − rate / 16) where rate is violent incidents per 1,000 residents (16 ≈ the pooled 90th percentile). Each neighborhood's own rate is first blended 60/40 with its neighbors' population-weighted rate — a light spatial pooling so a quiet block inside a rough district isn't scored as an island. Data-thin suburbs fall back to FBI agency-level rates.

Fixing the units problem

Walkability and greenery are population-weighted across census block groups rather than averaged over raw area, so a large, mostly-empty polygon isn't dragged down by land nobody lives on (a correction for the modifiable areal unit problem). Greenery counts only publicly usable open space. Concentrated public housing enters as a proximity disamenity — the "away from public housing" axis — plus a hard gate for tracts that are themselves majority public housing.

Walkshed maps

Each map is a true network catchment, not a circle. From the neighborhood centroid the pedestrian street network is walked outward along real streets, and every intersection reachable within an 800 m network distance (~10 minutes) is kept — an ego_graph truncated by travel distance. Tiles share a fixed ~1.7 km window, so two walksheds are directly comparable: a dense grid fills the frame, a cul-de-sac suburb doesn't.

Caveats

Scores and the cost axis are calibrated within each metro; effective rent is a real cross-metro dollar figure but is quality-adjusted from area rents, not a specific listing. The model screens neighborhoods — unit-level facts (true square footage, parking, laundry, soundproofing) are a manual per-listing step it deliberately doesn't attempt.
